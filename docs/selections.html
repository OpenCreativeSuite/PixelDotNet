Cutting out and protecting areas

Selections and masks let you work on one part of a picture and leave the rest untouched. This chapter shows how to cut a subject out, protect an area while editing, and refine the edges afterwards.

What a selection does

A selection is a stencil. When one exists, painting and effects only reach the pixels inside it. Everything outside is protected until you deselect.

Selections belong to the whole picture, not to one layer. You can make one selection and then paint inside it on several different layers. The moving dashed outline is often called “marching ants”. It is only a guide and never appears in the saved image.

These actions respect a selection:

  • Brush, eraser, line, gradient, and paint bucket strokes
  • Clone and healing strokes
  • Shapes drawn with the shape tools
  • Adjustments such as brightness, levels, and colour tools
  • Effects such as blur, sharpen, and pixelate
  • Fills and content-aware fill

Cut out a subject

Use when: you want a person or object on a transparent background. Quick Selection is the fastest route for a clean subject against a fairly simple background.

  1. Choose Quick Selection with A.
  2. Paint over the subject. Each dab adds the connected region of similar colour to the selection. Start with a small brush on a clear edge.
  3. Take areas back. Hold Alt, or turn on Subtract in the options bar, and paint over background that was caught by mistake.
  4. Copy and paste the subject. Press Ctrl+C, then Ctrl+V. The selected pixels become a new layer.
  5. Hide the original layer. Click its eye in the Layers panel to check the cut-out against transparency.
  6. Refine the edge. Add a mask to the new layer, then paint black on the mask to hide stray background and white to bring areas back. A soft brush gives a gentle fade.
Keep the original safe The pasted layer only contains the selected pixels. The original layer stays underneath, so you can hide it, move it, or come back to it at any time.

Magic Wand options

  • Tolerance decides how different a pixel can be and still match the colour you clicked. Low values select only nearly identical colours; high values select a wider range.
  • Contiguous keeps the selection to the connected area you clicked. Turn it off to select matching colours everywhere on the layer.
  • The wand reads the active layer. If the colour you want lives on another layer, select that layer first, or use Quick Selection, which reads the visible result.

Quick Selection options

  • Size controls the sampling brush. The selection often grows far beyond each dab.
  • Tolerance controls how eagerly the region grows. Lower it if the selection leaks into the background.
  • Subtract, or holding Alt, removes an area from the selection.

Add to and subtract from a selection

You are not limited to one shape. The modifier keys let you combine several areas into one selection:

ModifierResult
ShiftAdd the new area to the current selection.
CtrlAdd the new area to the current selection.
AltSubtract the new area from the current selection.
No modifierReplace the current selection with the new one.
When is the key read? For the marquee, ellipse, and lasso, hold the key before you start dragging. For the Magic Wand and Quick Selection, the key is checked when you click or start painting.

The options bar also has selection mode buttons for New, Add, Subtract, and Intersect, so you do not have to remember the keys.

Move or resize the selected pixels

Switch to the Move tool with V, then drag inside the selection. The selected pixels and the selection move together, leaving a transparent hole behind.

  • Rectangular and elliptical selections have resize handles. Drag a handle to stretch or shrink the selected pixels.
  • Freehand lasso selections can be moved but do not have resize handles.
  • Snapping can align the moved selection to guides or canvas edges. Turn it on from View → Toggle Snapping.
Moving pixels is destructive Moving a selection punches a hole in the active layer. If you only want to move the stencil without moving pixels, use the selection tool instead of the Move tool.

Masks: the flexible cut-out

A layer mask is a stencil attached to one layer. Unlike a selection, it stays with that layer, can be edited at any time, and never destroys the pixels. This is the best way to refine a cut-out.

Think of the mask as a black-and-white painting:

White reveals

Paint white on the mask to show the layer.

Black hides

Paint black on the mask to hide that part of the layer.

Grey fades

Paint grey for a partial reveal, like a frosted window.

Add a mask

  1. Click the mask button at the bottom of the Layers panel. The mask starts white and reveals the whole layer.
  2. Click the mask thumbnail so it has a light border. You are now painting on the mask, not on the layer pixels.
  3. Paint black to hide areas, or white to reveal them. Use grey for soft transitions.
Starting from a selection Select the area you want to hide, add the mask, then fill the selection with black on the mask. Press D for black and white, make sure the mask thumbnail is active, and choose Select → Fill with Foreground. Everything outside the selection stays revealed.

Click the layer thumbnail to return to painting on the layer itself. To remove a mask, right-click the layer in the Layers panel and choose Remove Mask.

Soft edges Use a soft brush on the mask for hair, clouds, and gentle fades. The layer pixels are never destroyed, so you can always paint the area back in.

Clip a layer to the one below

Clipping is another kind of stencil. A clipped layer only appears where the layer below has visible pixels. It is perfect for colouring line art or adding shading to a shape without painting outside its edges.

  1. Put the artwork you want to use as the boundary on one layer.
  2. Add a new layer above it and paint freely. It can cover the whole canvas.
  3. Turn on Clip for the upper layer, or right-click the layer and choose Clip to Below.
  4. The upper layer is now trimmed to the visible content of the layer below.

Clipped layers are marked in the Layers panel and are saved in project files.

Rulers, guides, and snapping

Guides are thin lines you place to line things up. They do not appear in the saved image.

  • View → Toggle Rulers shows the rulers around the canvas.
  • Drag from the top ruler to create a horizontal guide. Drag from the left ruler to create a vertical guide.
  • View → Add Horizontal Guide and Add Vertical Guide place guides at the centre.
  • View → Toggle Guides hides or shows all guides.
  • View → Toggle Snapping makes moved selections and layers snap to guides and canvas edges.
  • View → Clear Guides removes all guides at once.

View → Toggle Grid draws an eight-pixel grid over the canvas. Like guides, it is a visual aid and is never saved with the picture.
